# TODO: the brain problem (next problem to take)

## Motivation

The squeeze theorem (`lean/SquigglyLine/Theorem/Compression.lean`) holds for
any normed input space, with one real output. For `f : ℝ^d → ℝ` the squeezed
graph `x ↦ f(w·x)` is a hypersurface in ℝ^(d+1) with no thickness. As `w`
grows it comes within `ρ/w` of every point of the box, which is the
surface-fills-volume property of a folded cortex.

It is not a brain, because a function graph is a height field: one output per
input, so it cannot fold back over itself. A cortex has overhangs, and a
height field has none.

## Problem

State and prove a version of "closer, steeper, no better at the start" for a
thin surface that is not the graph of a function.

Setting to fix first:

   `S_w ⊂ ℝ^n`, not a graph `y = f(x)`.

   or to a choice of output coordinate (for example, a family `S_w`
   obtained from a base surface `S` by a rule that makes every fold
   `w` times finer).
3. Define "closer" as `sup over data points p of dist(p, S_w)`.

## Claims to settle (each proved, disproved, or marked open)

1. **Closer.** `dist(p, S_w) ≤ ρ / w` for every `p` in the box.
2. **Steeper.** Replace the slope bound by a bound on the second
   fundamental form, or by the area of `S_w` inside the box.

4. **Overhangs.** Say exactly which of 1 to 3 survive when `S_w` is
   not a height field, and which need it.

# TODO: the warp, adversarial examples and soft prompts

Opened 14 Nov 2026. Local note, not committed.

## Motivation

The warp (`exists_warp_of_coversWithin`, [book/04-nearness.md](book/04-nearness.md);
`claim_4_warp_fits_any_target` in `Compression.lean`) sends each input `x` to an input
`warp(x)` with `dist(x, warp(x)) ≤ reach` and `model(warp(x)) = target(x)`. The model is
frozen and only the place it is read changes. This is the shape of an adversarial example
(a small move of the input that changes the output) and of a soft prompt (a learned input
that steers a frozen model).

The repo has the adversarial bound from one side only: flipping a classifier of slope `K`
needs a move of at least `score / K` ([book/28](book/28-the-slope-of-a-network.md),
`NetworkSlope.lean`). The warp is the bound from the other side: a move of at most
`reach / w` always suffices for a squeezed model. Nothing in the repo says these are two
views of one fact.

The repo has nothing on soft prompts (searched 14 Nov 2026).

## Problem

Prove that the warp and the adversarial distance of a squeezed model are the same quantity
bracketed from two sides, and say what this does and does not say about soft prompts.

## Claims to settle (each proved, disproved, or marked open)

1. **Sandwich.** For a model squeezed by `w` and a target with values in the model's range,
   the smallest move that makes the model hit the target at `x` lies between
   `(vertical error) / (slope bound)` below and `reach / w` above. State it as one theorem
   combining `lipschitzWith_compress` (lower) and `CoversWithin.compress` (upper).
2. **Tightness.** For the sine, are both ends attained up to a constant? The
   chapter-4 experiment found the largest move equal to `π / frequency`, which suggests the
   upper end is sharp.
3. **Adversarial reading.** Restate the warp as: for a classifier built from a squeezed
   model, every input has an adversarial example within `reach / w`. Check that this
   follows with no new hypothesis beyond covering.
4. **Shared vs per-input (soft prompt).** A warp is one move per input. A soft prompt is
   one move shared by all inputs. Decide whether one shared shift (or a shift from a small
   family) can fit a target on many inputs at once, for the squeezed sine. Expect this to
   fail in general and mark it open or false if so.
5. **Existence vs finding.** The warp is only proved to exist. State whether gradient
   descent on the input finds it, or leave that explicitly open.

# TODO: break long proof steps into named `let` lines, in every proof

Opened 14 Nov 2026. Local note, not committed.

## Problem

Statements follow the style rule: one binder per line, each quantity a `let` on its own line.
Proof bodies do not. Steps inside `calc`, `have` and `exact` pack several function calls,
unfoldings and lemma names into one expression. Example, the `calc` in
`nearestDist_le_verticalDist` (`lean/SquigglyLine/Theorem/Sandwich.lean`, lines 43 to 48):

    calc GET_NEAREST_DISTANCE model data_point
        ≤ dist (EMBED_DATA_POINT data_point) footpoint :=
          Metric.infDist_le_dist_of_mem (footpoint_mem_curve model data_point)
      _ = GET_VERTICAL_ERROR model data_point := by
          simp [footpoint, dist_embed, GET_RESIDUAL_LOSS, Real.dist_eq,
            Real.sqrt_sq_eq_abs]

Neither step is over 80 columns, but each is a long expression that a reader has to parse
in one go: a nested call, a lemma applied to a lemma, and a `simp` with five rewrite rules.
That breaks the spirit of the rule, which is that every quantity gets a name and a line.
The 80-column limit (STYLE.md rule 6) is the floor, not the goal.

## Scope

All proofs, not this one. Measured on 14 Nov 2026 in `lean/SquigglyLine/`:

- 76 `calc` blocks.
- 507 lines over 80 columns, 243 over 100. (Many are trailing comments, which rule 6
  allows to overrun. The count of code lines over 80 is lower. Measure that separately.)

## Rule to write down

1. Name each distinct quantity in a proof with `let` (or `have` for a fact), one per line,
   before the step that uses it. Example: `let footpoint_distance := dist (EMBED_DATA_POINT
   data_point) footpoint`, then the calc step says `≤ footpoint_distance`.
2. A lemma applied to an argument that is itself an application gets the argument named first:
   `have footpoint_on_curve := footpoint_mem_curve model data_point` and then
   `Metric.infDist_le_dist_of_mem footpoint_on_curve`.
3. A `simp [...]` with more than two or three rewrite rules is split into separate `rw` or
   `simp only` steps, one per fact, each with a short comment or a named intermediate goal.
4. Each `calc` step fits on one line, apart from the justification, which goes on the next
   line. Where a step still needs more than about 60 columns, introduce another `let`.
5. The rule applies to every proof, including `private theorem`s and helper lemmas.

Add this to [lean/STYLE.md](lean/STYLE.md) and to the brief in [lean/PORTING.md](lean/PORTING.md)
so the porting pass produces it, not only a later clean-up.

## Cautions

- `PORTING.md` lines 53 to 54 and 71 to 74 warn that a `let` can break `rw` and `simp` in
  other files: a definition whose body other files `rw`/`simp` through must stay free of
  `let`s, and `rw` rejects a lemma whose statement is behind `let`s. So split proof steps,
  not definitions or statements that other files rewrite through, and rebuild after each file.
- `let` in a tactic proof introduces a local definition. If a later `simp` or `omega` does not
  see through it, use `set ... with` or `have h : x = ... := rfl`. Check per case.
- A rewrite is not the math. Behaviour must not change: `lake build` must pass after each
  file, and the proof must still close with no `sorry`.

# Addendum to "initialisation gives equal one-point marginals": target wording and the weaker claim

Opened 14 Nov 2026. Local note, not committed.

## What the proof should aim at

The earlier todo asks for equal one-point marginals at every width. That is probably false
at finite width for a real network (the output is a sum of products of random weights, not
Gaussian, and its distribution changes with width). What is likely true is that the mean
and the variance of the output do not change. Squared error reads only those two, because

    E[(label - output)^2] = label^2 - 2 * label * E[output] + E[output^2]

So the proof target is, for each input and every width:

    ∫ output ∂(network width).marginal input, output     = 0
    ∫ output ∂(network width).marginal input, output ^ 2 = initial_variance input

and from it:

    (network width).expected (GET_RESIDUAL_LOSS (· ^ 2)) data_distribution
      = (network other_width).expected (GET_RESIDUAL_LOSS (· ^ 2)) data_distribution

Not checked on a network yet, so the exact statement may need adjusting.

## Two different "equal" statements, keep them apart

| Property | Lean statement | Used by |
|---|---|---|
| same across inputs | `HAS_HOMOGENEOUS_MARGINALS network` | `marginal_compress_eq` |
| same across widths | `(network width).marginal = (network other_width).marginal` | the flat loss at the start |

The book paragraph is about the second. The lemma needs the first. With the usual
`1 / fan_in` scaling the output variance depends on the size of the input, so equal across
inputs needs an extra condition (for example inputs of a fixed length). Settle this before
writing.

## Decide: weaken the lemma, or say it does not apply

If only the first two moments are proved for a real network, the lemma
(`marginal_compress_eq`, which assumes equal marginals) is stronger than what the proof
gives. Either state a squared-error version of the lemma that assumes only equal first and
second moments, or have the book say the lemma is not what applies. Do not leave the
paragraph claiming that the lemma needs equal marginals and that the proof gives them.

## Target wording for book/05-the-blind-loss.md (lines 97 to 102)

Replace the current paragraph, once the one-hidden-layer proof exists, with something like:

> Standard initialisation of the weights is designed to keep the size of the output the same
> at every width. For squared error, the loss at the start reads only the mean and variance
> of the output. The book proves that for a [one-hidden-layer network with this scheme, at
> every width] the mean is zero and the variance is the same, so the expected squared loss
> at the start is the same at every width. For other losses and for deeper networks, this is
> measured, not proved.

Notes on the wording:

- "Of the weights": true of the weights, not the biases (`book/proposals/proposal-20261114.md`, items 1
  and 3). Say so or add a bias assumption.
- The last sentence stays. The proof covers squared error at one depth, so "consistent with,
  but does not explain" goes only for that case.

## Python check (run 14 Nov 2026)

One hidden layer, ReLU, no biases, weight variance `1 / fan_in` in both layers. The
first step on paper is the second moment:

    E[output^2] = sum_j E[v_j^2] * E[relu(w_j . x)^2]
                = width * (1 / width) * (||x||^2 / input_size) / 2
                = ||x||^2 / (2 * input_size)

The width cancels. The mean is zero because `v_j` has mean zero and is independent of the
first layer. The code below samples 200,000 networks per width and compares.

```python

One hidden layer, ReLU, no biases:
    output(x) = sum_j  v[j] * relu(w[j] . x)
    w[j, i] ~ Normal(0, 1 / input_size)     (variance 1 / fan_in)
    v[j]    ~ Normal(0, 1 / width)          (variance 1 / fan_in)

Paper prediction: mean 0 and variance ||x||^2 / (2 * input_size) at every width,
while the shape of the distribution (its kurtosis) changes with width.
"""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
input_size = 3
draw_count = 200_000


def sample_outputs(x, width):
    """output(x) for `draw_count` independent draws of all the weights."""
    first_layer = rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(1 / input_size), size=(draw_count, width, input_size))
    second_layer = rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(1 / width), size=(draw_count, width))
    hidden = np.maximum(first_layer @ x, 0.0)          # (draw_count, width)
    return (second_layer * hidden).sum(axis=1)         # (draw_count,)


def excess_kurtosis(values):
    centred = values - values.mean()
    return (centred ** 4).mean() / (centred ** 2).mean() ** 2 - 3.0


x = np.array([1.0, 2.0, -1.0])
predicted_variance = (x @ x) / (2 * input_size)
label = 0.7

print(f"predicted variance ||x||^2/(2d) = {predicted_variance:.4f}")
print(f"{'width':>6} {'mean':>8} {'variance':>9} {'kurtosis':>9} {'E[(label-out)^2]':>17}")
for width in (1, 2, 4, 16, 64, 256):
    out = sample_outputs(x, width)
    squared_loss = ((label - out) ** 2).mean()
    print(f"{width:>6} {out.mean():>8.4f} {out.var():>9.4f} "
          f"{excess_kurtosis(out):>9.3f} {squared_loss:>17.4f}")

print(f"\npredicted E[(label-out)^2] = label^2 + variance = {label**2 + predicted_variance:.4f}")

# The variance depends on the size of the input, not on the width.
for scale in (0.5, 1.0, 2.0):
    out = sample_outputs(scale * x, 16)
    print(f"input scaled by {scale}: variance {out.var():.4f}, "
          f"predicted {(scale**2) * predicted_variance:.4f}")
```

Output of the run:

```
predicted variance ||x||^2/(2d) = 1.0000
 width     mean  variance  kurtosis  E[(label-out)^2]
     1  -0.0009    1.0087    14.922            1.4999
     2   0.0019    1.0039     7.467            1.4912
     4   0.0013    1.0107     3.782            1.4988
    16  -0.0005    1.0021     0.958            1.4928
    64  -0.0001    1.0029     0.260            1.4930
   256  -0.0013    1.0000     0.065            1.4917

input scaled by 0.5: variance 0.2501, predicted 0.2500
input scaled by 1.0: variance 1.0044, predicted 1.0000
input scaled by 2.0: variance 4.0308, predicted 4.0000
```

What it shows:

- **Mean zero and variance `||x||^2 / (2 * input_size)` at every width.** This is the claim
  to prove in Lean. The expected squared loss is flat in width (1.49 to 1.50) and equals
  `label^2 + variance`.
- **The marginals are not equal across width.** The excess kurtosis falls from 14.9 at width 1
  to 0.07 at width 256, the output tending to a Gaussian. So "equal one-point marginals at
  every width" is false for this network, and the book paragraph must not claim it. Only the
  first two moments are the same.
- **The variance scales with `||x||^2`.** Doubling the input quadruples the variance. So the
  marginals are not the same across inputs either, and `HAS_HOMOGENEOUS_MARGINALS` fails for
  this network unless the inputs have a fixed length. This settles the "decide which
  equal" point above: the width claim holds, the across-inputs claim needs a condition.

Caveats: this is sampling, not proof. It covers one input dimension, ReLU, no biases, one
hidden layer. It does not test biases, depth, or other schemes.

## Lean statement this suggests (not written)

    theorem output_mean_is_zero
        (input : Inputs) (width : ℕ) :
        ∫ output ∂(network width).marginal input, output = 0

    theorem output_second_moment_is_width_free
        (input : Inputs) (width other_width : ℕ) :
        ∫ output ∂(network width).marginal input, output ^ 2
          = ∫ output ∂(network other_width).marginal input, output ^ 2

Proof idea: the output is a finite sum over hidden units, each term has mean zero given
the first layer, and the terms are independent; use linearity and independence of the two
layers (`IndepFun`), then the Gaussian second moment of a ReLU of a Gaussian
(`E[relu(g)^2] = variance / 2`, by symmetry). Check what Mathlib has on Gaussian moments
before planning.

A numpy version with the same shape rule as the 2D one, with `x` of shape `(..., d)` and
`frequency`, `phase` of shape `(K, M, d)`:

    terms  = amplitude * np.sin(angles).prod(axis=-1)     # (..., K, M)
    z      = terms.sum(axis=(-2, -1))

Check against a literal loop, as for 2D. Parameter count is `(2d + 1) * K * M`
(one amplitude, `d` frequencies, `d` phases per term). For `d = 2` this is `5 * K * M`,
as before.

stated for any normed input space, so the target is the same three theorems with `ℝ × ℝ`
replaced by the `d`-input space:

1. `marginal_egg_carton`: the output distribution at any input is the product-of-sines sum at

2. `HAS_HOMOGENEOUS_MARGINALS`, as a corollary.
3. `COMPRESS` of the sum is the same sum with every frequency scaled.

# TODO (new proof): the cost of closeness, "thinking harder takes more energy"

Opened 14 Nov 2026. Local note, not committed. A new result, not just a restatement.

## The plain-language claim

> Thinking harder takes more energy, but it lets you fit harder data.

Make this a theorem. It also gives the book a single sentence for why a squiggly curve is
"steeper" and still worth having, and it lands next to the slew-rate picture (the steepness a
circuit must support to draw the curve).

## Terms

- **Effort** is the steepness `K` of the curve: `amplitude * frequency` for a sine, the
  `slope_limit` of `LipschitzWith slope_limit model` in general. Its square `K^2` is the
  "energy-like" quantity. Which of the two is called energy is a modelling choice; say so
  in the statement and the text.
- **Harder data** is a smaller target distance `ε`, for a point with vertical error `v > ε`.
  A larger `v` means the label is further from the curve, so the curve has to move more.

## The statement

For a data point with vertical error `v > ε > 0`:

    √(v² / ε² − 1)   ≤   K_needed   ≤   amplitude * PI / ε

- **Lower end (necessary).** If a `K`-Lipschitz curve has `nearest_distance ≤ ε`, then
  `K ≥ √(v² / ε² − 1)`. This is the existing lower bound
  `verticalDist_le_of_lipschitz` (`Sandwich.lean`) solved for `K`:
  `v ≤ √(1 + K²) * nearest_distance ≤ √(1 + K²) * ε`.
- **Upper end (sufficient).** A random-phase sine of amplitude `A` with `frequency = PI / ε`
  has `nearest_distance ≤ ε` (when the label is in the band) and slope `A * PI / ε`. This is
  `sineWave_nearestDist_bounds` (`SineCloser.lean`), upper bound `PI / frequency`.

Both ends scale as `1 / ε`. Halve the distance you want, double the steepness, quadruple
`K²`. Fitting twice as precisely costs four times the energy-like quantity.

Proposed Lean shape (names to follow the naming rules, `snake_case`, no clipped words):

    theorem steepness_needed_for_closeness
        {slope_limit : SlopeLimit}
        (slope_is_bounded : LipschitzWith slope_limit model)
        (data_point : DataPoint Inputs)
        (target_distance : ℝ)
        (vertical_error_exceeds_target : target_distance < GET_VERTICAL_ERROR model data_point)
        (nearest_distance_is_within_target :
          GET_NEAREST_DISTANCE model data_point ≤ target_distance) :
        √((GET_VERTICAL_ERROR model data_point / target_distance) ^ 2 - 1) ≤ slope_limit

    theorem sine_reaches_target_with_steepness
        (amplitude target_distance : ℝ) (target_is_positive : 0 < target_distance)
        (data_point : DataPoint ℝ) (label_is_in_band : ...) :
        ∃ frequency, nearest_distance (sine amplitude frequency phase) data_point ≤ target_distance
          ∧ amplitude * frequency ≤ amplitude * PI / target_distance

The first is a few lines on top of `verticalDist_le_of_lipschitz`. The second repackages
`sineWave_nearestDist_bounds` with `frequency = PI / target_distance`. So most of the work
is the statement and the book text, not a long proof. Check for an edge case at
`vertical_error = target_distance` (the square root of 0) and for `slope_limit` as a
`ℝ≥0`.

## The catch to state, in the same section

Energy buys **nearness, not a better start**. The vertical error, and so the loss at the
start (`marginal_compress_eq`), does not improve however steep the curve gets. So the honest
version of the slogan is: thinking harder takes more energy and lets you reach harder data,
but reaching it is not the same as the loss going down. Moving to the point you reach is the
warp (`exists_warp_of_coversWithin`).

## Questions to settle

1. **Which is "energy": `K` or `K²`?** For an oscillator the maximum kinetic energy is
   `∝ (amplitude * frequency)²`, which supports `K²`. A capacitive circuit's average power is
   `∝ amplitude² * frequency`, which is neither. Pick one for the book and justify it, and
   do not claim the theorem proves a physical power law. It proves a bound on slope.
2. **Per point or per dataset?** The statement above is for one data point. A version for a
   whole dataset (`N` points within `ε`) needs more, and may bring in the covering-number
   view: the number of terms needed grows with how many wiggles the data has.
3. **Is the lower end tight?** The sine's `amplitude * PI / ε` and the general
   `√(v² / ε² − 1)` differ by the factor `amplitude * PI / v`. Show whether a better curve
   than the sine closes it, or whether the sine is optimal up to a constant.
4. **"Harder data" beyond `v`.** Here harder means farther labels or tighter `ε`. A second
   sense is data whose labels change faster along the input (a higher-frequency target).
   Decide whether the book needs that and, if so, how it differs from the first.

## Where it goes

- A new short section in [book/06-the-sine.md](book/06-the-sine.md) after "The statement" or
  in chapter 3 (the slope sandwich). Possibly a new chapter if the dataset version is done.
- A new file in `lean/SquigglyLine/Theorem/` (next to `Sandwich.lean`), then the
  supporting-proofs table in `docs/supporting-proofs.md`.
- A figure: steepness against `1 / ε`, with the lower and upper curves and the sine on
  top. Add to the diagram todo for chapter 6 if it fits there.

## Constraints from the project rules

- Connects to the squiggly line directly (it uses the sandwich), so it fits "no standalone
  proofs".
- Established ideas only: Lipschitz bounds and covering are established. The energy reading
  is a modelling frame, not a new physical claim.

# TODO (new proofs): sorting, pruning and noise in the learned-frequency sum

Opened 14 Nov 2026. Local note, not committed.

## The idea

Textbook Fourier: a signal is a sum of sines over a grid of frequencies, low to high. To
filter noise you drop the highest frequencies and keep the rest. In the squiggly sum
(`z = sum over k of a[k] * sin(w[k] * x + p[k])`, with the egg-carton and hyper-egg-carton
versions in more inputs) the frequencies `w[k]` are free parameters, not fixed on a grid.
The proposal: start the `w[k]` at random, let them separate, sort by `w[k]`, then throw out
the low-amplitude terms and the very high-frequency terms as noise and overfitting.

## What holds and what does not (assessed 14 Nov 2026)

1. **"Random `w[k]` dodge each other": probably false as stated.** K independent uniform
   frequencies in `[0, W]` have a smallest gap of order `W / K^2`. The probability that all
   gaps exceed `δ` is `(1 - (K - 1) * δ / W)^K`. So near-duplicate pairs are likely once
   `K` is large. Two near-equal frequencies give two nearly identical functions, and the fit
   cancels them with large opposite amplitudes. That is a blow-up, not repulsion. Whether
   gradient descent separates them is **open** (my expectation: it does not; untested).
2. **The textbook case is the special case without this problem.** Frequencies on the fixed
   grid `k * 2 * PI / T` are orthogonal, amplitudes follow from Parseval, and the order is
   built in. A stratified init (one frequency per grid cell, plus a small jitter) guarantees
   separation and is the natural fix.
3. **"Sort by `w[k]`": fine.** The sum is unchanged by permuting the terms, so sorting gives
   a canonical form. It fails only on exact ties, which have probability zero under a
   continuous init.
4. **Pruning: use `|a[k]| * w[k]`, not either alone.** That product is term `k`'s slope
   contribution (the slew rate it needs). Dropping a set `S` of terms changes the output by
   at most `sum over S of |a[k]|` everywhere, and lowers the slope bound by
   `sum over S of |a[k]| * w[k]`.
5. **"High frequency is noise" is an assumption about the data, not a theorem.** It holds when
   the signal spectrum decays and the noise is flat. Past the crossover frequency the signal
   drops below the noise. On an orthogonal grid the standard threshold is about
   `σ * √(2 * log K)` (Donoho and Johnstone). If the target has fast variation, pruning
   underfits.

## Proofs and experiments, in order

### A. Pruning bound and the overfitting cap (easy, good first bite)

Statement, for a sum of `K` terms and a set `S` of dropped terms:

    sup over x of |full(x) - pruned(x)|  ≤  sum over S of |a[k]|
    slope_bound(pruned)                  =  slope_bound(full) - sum over S of |a[k]| * w[k]   (upper bound form: ≤)

and from the existing lower bound `verticalDist_le_of_lipschitz` (`Sandwich.lean`):

    nearest_distance(pruned, point)  ≥  vertical_error(pruned, point) / √(1 + K_pruned^2)

so pruning high-frequency terms provably limits how closely the model can pass to a noisy
point. This is the cost-of-closeness result (see "The cost of closeness" above) used as a
noise cap. Lean: triangle inequality over a `Finset` sum, `LipschitzWith` of a finite sum
of sines (`lipschitzWith_sine` in `SineCloser.lean` is the one-term case). Check how
`LipschitzWith` composes under sums in Mathlib.

### B. Random frequencies do not dodge (classical, medium)

Prove the min-gap statement: for `K` i.i.d. uniform points on `[0, W]`, the probability that
all pairwise gaps are at least `δ` is `(1 - (K - 1) * δ / W)^K` when `(K - 1) * δ ≤ W`.
Consequence: collisions are likely once `K^2 * δ ≳ W`. Needs measure theory on order
statistics, which Mathlib may not have in this form. Check before planning. Could be stated
first for `K = 2` (the probability that two frequencies are closer than `δ` is
`1 - (1 - δ/W)^2`), which is short.

### C. Identifiability and conditioning (established, hard)

Ingham's inequality (1936, sharpened by Montgomery and Vaughan): if the frequencies are
separated by at least `γ` and the window has length `T > 2 * PI / γ`, the sines form a Riesz
sequence, so amplitudes are determined stably and sorting is meaningful. Not in Mathlib as
far as I know. Treat as a stretch goal, or cite it as a recalled result in the book
(**recalled**, as the book labels such things).

### D. Do the frequencies repel during training? (experiment first)

A numpy experiment in the style of `init_check.py`: fit a sum of `K` free-frequency sines to
noisy data by gradient descent, from a random init and from a stratified init. Record:

- the minimum gap between sorted frequencies over training,
- the largest `|a[k]|` (blow-up shows as large, opposite-sign pairs),
- the fit error with and without pruning by `|a[k]| * w[k]`.

No proof until the experiment says what is true. Cheap, and decides whether D is worth a
theorem.

# TODO (analogy, for the diagram and the book): voltage, current, resistance and power

Opened 14 Nov 2026. Local note, not committed. An analogy to explain the bounds, not a
theorem. Goes with the "cost of closeness" section and the chapter-6 diagram.

## The question

If power and voltage are in the picture, what are current and resistance? There are two
mappings. Both are analogies. They should not be mixed in one paragraph.

## Mapping 1: the signal view (a capacitive load)

Treat the curve as a voltage `V(t) = amplitude * sin(frequency * t)` driving a capacitor `C`.

| Circuit | Squiggly line |
|---|---|
| voltage `V` | `amplitude` (the swing) |
| frequency `f` (clock speed) | `frequency` |
| current `I = C * dV/dt` | the slope, peak `C * amplitude * frequency` (the slew rate) |
| impedance `V / I = 1 / (C * frequency)` | `1 / frequency`, the length scale (the upper bound is `PI / frequency`) |
| power `P` | `C * amplitude^2 * frequency` on average, the usual `C * V^2 * f` |

The amplitude cancels in `V / I`, so the "resistance" here is the length scale of one wave,
the same `PI / frequency` that bounds the nearest distance from above.

## Mapping 2: the fit view (Ohm's law for closeness)

This one matches the repo's theorems more directly.

| Circuit | Squiggly line |
|---|---|
| voltage `V` | `vertical_error` `v`, the gap to close |
| current `I` | the steepness `K` of the curve |
| resistance `R = V / I` | the distance `ε` you must travel to close the gap |
| power `P = I * V` | `K * v`, steepness times gap |

Why it fits: the lower bound `nearest_distance ≥ vertical_error / √(1 + K²)`
(`verticalDist_le_of_lipschitz`) is Ohm's law, `distance = gap / steepness` for large `K`.
The cost-of-closeness result then reads `K_needed ≈ v / ε`, which is `I = V / R`. A
steeper curve is a lower-resistance path: it closes the same gap over a shorter distance.

## Caveats to put in the text

- It is an analogy. The theorem proves bounds on a slope and a distance. It says nothing
  about power, charge or heat.
- The power law differs between mappings (`C * V^2 * f` against `K * v`). Do not claim one
  power formula for both.
- `√(1 + K²)` is `K` only for steep curves. Say "for steep curves" when writing
  `distance ≈ gap / steepness`.
- Pick one mapping per section. Mapping 2 is the one to use next to the sandwich and the
  cost-of-closeness theorem. Mapping 1 is the one to use for "clock speed" and the slew rate.

## Task

1. Decide where each mapping goes (suggestion: Mapping 2 in the cost-of-closeness section,
   Mapping 1 in the chapter-6 slew-rate note).
2. Add the small table to the text, with one sentence naming the analogy as an analogy.
3. If the diagram todo for chapter 6 adds a figure, label the orange line (`vertical_error`)
   as the "voltage" and the steepness triangle as the "current", only if that does not
   crowd the figure. Optional.

# TODO: interpretations and analogies for `reach` and `squeeze_factor` in chapter 7

Opened 14 Nov 2026. Local note, not committed.

Page: https://ghost---shadow.github.io/squiggly-line-theorem/07-any-function.html
Source: [book/07-any-function.md](book/07-any-function.md).

## Problem

"The idea" (lines 8 to 36) uses `reach` and `squeeze_factor` as if the reader already knew
them: "If the model covered the labels within `reach`, the squeezed one covers them within
`reach / squeeze_factor`." Nowhere does the page say what `reach` is. It is a name this
project chose (not a Lean or Mathlib term; see `COVERS_WITHIN` in
`lean/SquigglyLine/Theorem/Oscillation.lean`). `squeeze_factor` is explained only by a
formula. The page needs plain-words readings and a few analogies, each marked as an
analogy.

## Task

Add a short section after "The idea" (suggested title: "Reading `reach` and the squeeze
factor"), or work the sentences into "The idea" itself. Cover the points below.

### What `reach` is

- **A distance along the input axis.** How far you may have to walk, sideways, from where you
  stand to find an input where the model gives the label you want. It is the largest such
  distance over all inputs and all labels in the range.
- **Not the vertical error, and not the nearest distance.** Those are distances to the
  curve in the plane. Reach is horizontal only. This is why squeezing shrinks reach and
  leaves the vertical error alone.
- **Worked number.** `sin(x)`: from `x = 0`, the label 0.5 is at `x ≈ 0.52`, the label 1 at
  `x = PI / 2`. The worst case is a peak to a trough, `PI`. So `reach = PI`, half a period.
  Squeezed by 10, every distance is divided by 10.
- **The search window is a full period.** The window `[x - reach, x + reach]` is `2 * PI /
  frequency` wide. A one-sided half period misses labels (stand at a rising zero crossing:
  only the non-negative labels are in reach). Most labels appear twice in the window, once
  rising and once falling, and the nearer one is the answer. The falling half is not wasted:
  a bounded continuous function must come back down.
- **For other functions it is not a period.** The fold network has no period and a reach of
  `1 / 2^L`. So do not call it the period (and `cycle_length` would be wrong by a factor of 2
  for sines). Say "for a sine, half a period".

### Analogies for `reach` (say each is an analogy)

- **Search radius:** you hold an answer and look for the question that gives it. Reach is
  how far you scan. Caveat: the search work in probes does not shrink with squeezing,
  because the wiggles are also narrower. You scan about one wiggle either way.
- **Nudge size:** the warp (`exists_warp_of_coversWithin`) moves each input by at most
  `reach`. Same shape as an adversarial example, or a soft prompt on a frozen model. See the
  warp and adversarial todo.
- **Ohm's law:** with the fit-view mapping (voltage is the vertical error, current the
  steepness, resistance the distance), reach plays the worst-case distance. See the
  voltage, current and resistance todo.

### What `squeeze_factor` is

- **A zoom on the input axis.** The squeezed model reads the original at `squeeze_factor ×
  input`. Zooming out on a map: the same terrain, every distance shorter.
- **Playback speed.** A record played at twice the speed has the same notes, each half as
  long, at a higher pitch. Squeezing by 2 does the same to a curve. It also explains "no
  better": the average loudness of a recording does not change with the speed it is
  played at, and neither does the distribution of values the model outputs at one input.
- **Frequency and clock speed.** The squeeze factor is the sine's frequency. Higher is
  squigglier, and steeper by the same factor (the slew-rate picture, see the cost of
  closeness todo).
- **The other names in the table "One dial, many names":** stride, bump width, temperature.
  One sentence each would make the table less abstract, as one analogy per row.

### What each analogy gets wrong (state it in the text)

- Playback speed: a record has a start and an end. The model is defined on all inputs.
- Search radius: the model does not search. The statement is only that a hit exists.
- Circuit and "energy": the theorem proves bounds on a slope and a distance. It says nothing
  about power, charge or heat.

# TODO (addendum to the pruning todo): L2 regularisation, slope and reach

Opened 14 Nov 2026. Local note, not committed. Extends "sorting, pruning and noise in the
learned-frequency sum". Related book chapter: `book/20-weight-decay-and-early-stopping.md`.

## The question

L2 regularisation shrinks weights. Weights contribute to amplitude, amplitude contributes to
slope. Does L2 therefore increase `reach`, and is that bad?

## Answer so far (reasoned, not yet proved or run)

For a sine with the label in its band:

- slope = `amplitude * frequency`
- reach = `PI / frequency` (the amplitude does not appear)

So it depends on which weights shrink:

1. **L2 on the amplitude** (output weights): slope falls, reach unchanged, provided the
   labels still fit in the band `[-amplitude, amplitude]`. If the amplitude shrinks below the
   labels, the model cannot hit them and the reach is infinite.
2. **L2 on the frequency** (roughly the first-layer weights that scale the input): slope
   falls and reach grows, as `PI / frequency` grows when the frequency shrinks.
3. **Both:** for an amplitude fixed by the labels, a slope budget `K` forces
   `frequency ≤ K / amplitude`, so reach `≥ PI * amplitude / K`. This is the cost-of-closeness
   squeeze read backwards: less steepness allowed, larger reach.

A larger reach is bad for fitting everything, noise included, and that is the point of
regularising. A lower slope also caps how closely the model can pass to a noisy point (the
overfitting cap in the pruning todo, A). So L2 trades closeness for smoothness. The repo
already says this in another form: `docs/insights.md` line 66 sorts regularisers as charges on
slope.

Link to the Fourier filter idea: L2 on the frequencies `w[k]` pulls them toward zero and
favours low frequencies, a soft low-pass filter. L2 on the amplitudes `a[k]` is soft
amplitude pruning.

## Claims to settle

1. **Statement for the sine.** With `amplitude` fixed and a slope budget `K`, show
   `reach ≥ PI * amplitude / K` for the random-phase sine. This is `sineWave_nearestDist_bounds`
   solved for the frequency, and it is the same inequality as the cost-of-closeness lower end.
   Small, builds on existing theorems.
2. **Which weights are the frequency?** In the sum-of-sines model the `w[k]` are parameters,
   so L2 on them is literal. In a real network the first-layer weights act like frequencies
   only loosely. State this as a modelling step and do not claim it is proved.
3. **The infinite-reach case.** Write down the condition under which a shrinking amplitude
   makes `COVERS_WITHIN` fail (the label leaves the band). It is a clean boundary and worth a
   sentence in the book.
4. **L2 as a charge on slope.** Chapter 20 and `Regularisers.lean` prove statements for a
   linear model. Check whether the slope statement here extends, or stays a linear-model
   result. Do not claim more than the Lean covers.

(b) frequencies only, (c) both. For each, record the final slope bound `sum of |a[k]| * w[k]`,

# TODO (new project): a polarizer-plate optical classifier for MNIST, in torch, then in plastic

Opened 15 Nov 2026. Local note, not committed. A simulation project with theory attached.
Builds on [book/12-polarizers.md](book/12-polarizers.md),
[docs/networks/three-polarizers.md](docs/networks/three-polarizers.md) and the
polarizer section of `README.md` (lines 575 to 612: "What is proved", "What is missing").

## The picture

Shine light shaped like an MNIST digit onto the input side of a stack of plates. Each plate
has a pattern (a polarizer angle at every point). The light passes through the stack and ends
on a screen as a bright dot at one of ten places. Show a 7 and the dot appears at the "7
mark". The computation happens as the light travels, with no power spent on the plates.

## Ground rules from what the repo already says

- **Proved for plates** (`ThreePolarizers.lean`, `Polarizer.lean`): a plate is a projection,
  Malus's law (`malus`), a quarter turn over `N` plates keeps at least `1 - π²/(8N)` of the
  light (`quarter_turn_arrives`).
- **Missing** (`README.md`): the nonlinearity. A stack of polarizers is one linear map. The
  detector reads brightness, which is the square of the field: one nonlinear step, at the end.
- **Existing work** (**recalled**, as `README.md` says): optical networks from printed
  diffracting plates and interferometer meshes (Lin et al. 2018; Shen et al. 2017). The
  Lin et al. network is the closest relative: diffractive layers, a detector with ten
  regions, MNIST as a test. Read it (or its abstract) before building, and say in any write-up
  what is and is not new here.

## Two design facts to settle first (they change the plan)

1. **Plates that only turn polarizer angles do not mix pixels.** A patterned polarizer acts
   on each point separately. A stack of them is a separate chain at each pixel, so output
   spot `j` would depend only on input pixel `j`. To classify, light from different pixels must
   meet. That needs **propagation between plates** (diffraction over a gap, or lenses). So the
   simulation must include free-space propagation, not only plates.
2. **Polarizer plates cannot bend light.** A polarizer changes amplitude and polarization
   direction, not phase. Caustics (below) come from phase. A **patterned half-wave plate**
   turns a local axis angle `θ(x)` into a phase `2θ(x)` for circularly polarized light (the
   geometric or Pancharatnam-Berry phase, **recalled**), and loses no light. So "angle as the
   weight" can be kept, but with half-wave plates for steering and polarizers for the loss
   term. Decide which plate the project means: absorbing polarizer, lossless half-wave
   plate, or both in alternation.

## Phase 0: validate the building blocks (small, do first)

Reproduce numbers already in the repo, in torch, so we know the simulator is right:

- Malus: passed length `amplitude * cos(angle)`; crossed plate passes 0 (checked in the
  chapter to `6e-17`).
- Three polarizers: horizontal, 45°, vertical gives intensity `0.25`; without the middle
  plate, `0`.
- A quarter turn over `N` plates: length `cos(π/(2N))^N`, table in
  `docs/networks/three-polarizers.md` (N = 2: 0.50, 5: 0.78, 10: 0.88, 30: 0.96, 90: 0.986).

## Phase 1: the simulation

**Model (vector, coherent).** The field at each point is a complex 2-vector `E` (x and y
polarization), shape `(batch, 2, H, W)`.

- **Plate** with axis angle `θ(x)`: `E ← u uᵀ E`, with `u = (cos θ, sin θ)`. This is a
  projector, applied at every pixel.
- **Gap of length `d`:** each component propagates with the angular-spectrum method (FFT, the
  transfer function `exp(i · 2π · d · √(1/λ² − fx² − fy²))`, evanescent waves cut off).
- **Input:** the MNIST image as amplitude, uniformly polarized (an input polarizer), padded to
  a larger grid so diffraction does not wrap around.
- **Readout:** intensity `|E|²` summed over both components, then integrated inside ten
  windows (the "marks"). Prediction is the window with the most light.
- **Loss:** cross-entropy on the window energies, divided by their total and scaled by a
  temperature (see chapters 14 and 15 on temperature, a slope dial).

**What to run:**

1. Number of plates `N` against test accuracy (the repo's own "more plates" axis).
2. Gap `d` against accuracy: too small and pixels do not mix, too large and it blurs.
3. Resolution (pixel pitch) and wavelength, kept in physical units.
4. Plate type: polarizer only, half-wave only, alternating.
5. **Light budget:** fraction of input light that reaches the detector, against accuracy.
   Large turns between plates lose light; the repo's `π²/(8N)` result says small turns are
   cheap. Add a penalty on the turn between plates and plot accuracy against transmitted light.
6. Baselines on the same data: a linear classifier on pixels, and the same with a squared
   readout. These are the expected ceiling (next section).

**Cost:** CPU torch is installed (2.14.0, CPU only). A few hundred pixels per side and 3 to 5
plates is fine. Keep the grid small first.

**Data:** `torchvision.datasets.MNIST` downloads from the internet. **Ask before
downloading**, and say the size (about 11 MB) and the source when asking.

## Theory and proofs (established ideas only; each connects to the squiggly line)

Everything here is a candidate. None is proved yet. Statements are marked as to what I expect.

### T1. A pixelwise plate stack is a diagonal operator (easy, Lean-able)

A product of per-pixel 2×2 projectors is, at each pixel, a rank-one map
`u_N (Π cos(θ_{k+1} - θ_k)) u_1ᵀ`. So without propagation the stack is diagonal in space and
output `j` depends only on pixel `j`. Extends `three_polarizers` and `malus`. Justifies design
fact 1.

### T2. What the optical classifier can compute (easy)

With coherent light, plates and propagation are linear in the field, so the output field is
`T x` for a matrix `T`. The window energy is `Σ_{p in window j} |(T x)_p|² = xᵀ Q_j x`,
with `Q_j = T_jᴴ T_j` positive semi-definite and of rank at most the number of pixels in
window `j`. So the class score is a **quadratic form**. This is a degree-2 feature lift
(chapter 11, "the lift"): the classifier is a linear classifier on the products
`x_a * x_b`, with constrained weights. It gives a ceiling: no feature of degree above 2, and a
rank limit per class. Statement to prove: scores are `xᵀ Q_j x` with `Q_j ⪰ 0`, and
`rank Q_j ≤ window size`. Expected accuracy is near a linear classifier with a squared
readout, not near a convolutional network (**expected, untested**: check it in Phase 1).

### T3. Squeeze and the ray map: closer, steeper, no better, in optics (the new result)

In geometric optics a stack of phase plates and gaps defines a **ray map** `y = f(x)` from
the input position to the output position (one dimension first). Two textbook facts
(**recalled**):

- **Energy conservation:** the intensity at `y` is `Σ over x with f(x) = y of I_in(x) / |f'(x)|`.
- **Caustics are folds:** where `f'(x) = 0` the intensity diverges (like `1/√(y - y_c)`),
  and the total energy stays finite.

Then the three claims of the squiggly line read as:

- **Closer:** the ray map `f` covers the output positions within `reach` (`COVERS_WITHIN`,
  with the ray map as the model). Squeezing, `f(w · x)`, covers them within `reach / w`.
- **Steeper:** the slope of `f` is multiplied by `w`.
- **No better:** the total light is unchanged (a change of variables), so the average
  brightness does not improve. The intensity per ray falls by `1/w` as the map gets steeper.

So steeper maps put light nearer to every output spot, with each ray dimmer, and with total
power fixed. Proof route: the change-of-variables formula for a piecewise monotone map
(Mathlib has `MeasureTheory.integral_image_eq_integral_abs_deriv_smul`; check the exact name),
applied to `COMPRESS`. First case: one input, `f` monotone on pieces. This is a short
statement and is the best candidate for a new proof in this project. Mark the physical
reading as an analogy in the text, and the change-of-variables part as proved.

The fold maps of `TentCloser.lean` are the tent map's ray-map analogue: each fold is a
caustic. Say so, and check whether the `1 / 2^L` result has an optical reading (many folds,
every output spot within `1 / 2^L` of every input).

### T4. Light budget (medium)

Per pair of plates the intensity transmitted is `cos²(Δθ)`. Show the trade: a weight that
differs from "identity" by `ε` costs a turn of about `ε` and loses about `ε²/2`, the same
shape as `turn_in_steps_keeps`. State the exact trade between achievable weights and total
transmission. Reuses `quarter_turn_arrives`.

## Caustics, optimised with torch

Idea (from the user): shape the plates so the light from each digit class **focuses**, as a
caustic, onto its own mark on the screen.

- **Forward model, geometric:** the ray map `f` from plate phases. A phase `φ(x)` bends a ray
  by `∇φ / k`. For a half-wave plate with axis angle `θ(x)` the phase is `2θ(x)`, so
  `∇φ = 2∇θ`: **the angle map `θ` steers the light.** Trace rays through the stack,
  differentiably.
- **Forward model, wave:** the angular-spectrum model of Phase 1. Geometric rays are for
  design intuition and a good initialisation. The wave model gives the real answer, including
  the diffraction that smooths the caustic (the intensity near a fold becomes an Airy-type
  pattern, **recalled**).
- **Objective:** for each class, the energy that lands in that class's window, minus the
  energy that lands elsewhere. Optimise the plate angles by gradient descent.
- **Related, recalled:** computational caustic design (optimal-transport and Poisson-solve
  methods for freeform lenses that focus light into a target image). Check what is known
  before claiming anything.

Questions to answer:

1. Does a caustic-style initialisation (rays aimed at the class marks) train better than a
   random one? Compare accuracy and steps.
2. How much of the focusing needs phase (half-wave plates)? Polarizer-only plates cannot
   bend rays, so test the polarizer-only stack for how much it can focus at all.
3. Is a caustic robust? A fold concentrates light, but it moves with small plate errors.
   Test sensitivity to angle error and gap error, because this decides whether it can be
   printed.

## Phase 2: a practical way to print the plates

Think about this after the simulation says which plates are needed. Ideas, all **unchecked**
and mostly **recalled**. Each needs a source check before it goes in a write-up.

1. **Patterned liquid-crystal alignment.** Photo-align a layer so the local axis angle
   `θ(x)` varies across the plate. With dye-doped liquid crystal it can act as a pixelated
   polarizer, and without dye as a patterned half-wave plate (the geometric-phase optics
   above). Resolution of a few micrometres is reported (**recalled**). Likely the best match
   to "an angle at every pixel".
2. **Micro-polarizer arrays.** Polarization cameras carry on-chip wire-grid polarizers at
   four angles (0°, 45°, 90°, 135°), **recalled**. Four levels is a coarse but real
   baseline: train with the angle quantised to 4 levels.
3. **Direct laser writing in glass** (femtosecond nanogratings give a local slow axis,
   **recalled**). These are waveplates, so this fits the half-wave route, not absorbing
   polarizers.
4. **3D-printed diffractive plates** (the terahertz route in Lin et al., **recalled**): the
   pattern is thickness, so phase only, no polarizer angle. A long wavelength makes the
   features printable. Not the polarizer idea, but the lowest-risk way to test the optical
   classifier itself.
5. **A low-cost table-top demo (untested idea).** Use a spatial light modulator or an LCD as
   a programmable plate and a polarization camera or ten photodiodes as the readout, to test
   the design before fabricating anything.

Steps whichever route is chosen:

1. Train in torch with the fabrication limits built in: angle quantised to the levels the
   process gives, noise on angles, small shifts and tilts between plates, plate spacing
   fixed by the stack.
2. Export each plate as an image or layout file of angles.
3. Make the plates, stack with spacers, add an input polarizer, a digit mask or screen, and
   a detector with ten regions.
4. Test with printed or displayed MNIST digits and compare with the simulator.

Practical constraints to check: pixel pitch against wavelength (features smaller than the
wavelength do not behave as pixels), gap against `pitch² / wavelength` (how far light spreads
and mixes), total transmitted light against detector noise, alignment accuracy between plates
(a few micrometres is hard), and a **coherent** source (a laser, or a filtered LED). An LED
or an ordinary screen is incoherent, and then the model changes: intensities add instead of
fields, the weights become non-negative, and the ceiling is lower (**untested**; worth a
separate simulation).

## Subsection 2: "A reminder: the Fourier transform" (a primer for readers who have not met it)

Place: after the reach subsection and before "The statement", or after "Why it is true" if it
interrupts the flow. Keep it short. Mark it as optional background.

Why chapter 7 needs it: the chapter says "the sine is the special case". A reader who knows
that every function is built from sines sees at once why the sine is a good representative,
and why squeezing a function is the same as raising every frequency in it.

Contents:

- **The idea in one paragraph.** A sound, a signal or a curve can be written as a sum of
  sine waves of different frequencies, each with its own amplitude and phase. The list of
  those amplitudes and phases is the Fourier transform. Low frequencies are the slow, smooth
  part of the function. High frequencies are the fast wiggles.
- **Periodic functions: the Fourier series.** `f(x) = sum over k of a[k] * sin(k * x + p[k])`.
  Say what is needed for it to work (the function repeats, and is nice enough), and that at a
  jump the sum overshoots (the Gibbs effect), as one sentence.
- **Any function: the Fourier transform.** The sum becomes an integral over all frequencies.
  One sentence, no formula beyond what the chapter needs.
- **The bridge to squeezing (the reason it is here).** Squeezing a sum of sines by `s`
  multiplies every frequency by `s` and leaves the amplitudes and phases alone:

      squeeze(s)( sum of a[k] * sin(w[k] * x + p[k]) ) = sum of a[k] * sin(s * w[k] * x + p[k])

  So in the Fourier picture "squeeze" is "multiply every frequency by the squeeze factor",
  and "squigglier" is "more weight at high frequency". This is exact and short to prove; it is
  the same identity as `compress_egg_carton_eq` in the egg-carton draft
  (`lean/scratch/EggCarton.lean`).
- **Noise filtering, the textbook use.** Drop the highest frequencies and keep the rest. One
  sentence, with a pointer to the pruning todo (what the squiggly line says about dropping
  terms, using `|a[k]| * w[k]`).
- **Where the book meets it again:** chapter 6 (the sine), chapter 11 (random Fourier
  features, the lift), the weight-decay chapter (low-pass reading of L2 on frequencies), and
  the polarizer classifier (diffraction is a Fourier transform: the angular-spectrum method
  multiplies each frequency by a phase).

What is proved and what is recalled (the book's labels):

  textbook mathematics. Mathlib has Fourier series on the circle (`AddCircle`,
  `fourierCoeff`, Parseval), and the repo already imports `Mathlib.Analysis.Fourier.AddCircle`,
  but that is the library's result, not this book's. Say so, and do not re-prove it.

Do not claim more than the primer needs: this is a reminder, not a chapter. A reader who

# TODO: chapter 8, the squiggly line as the side view of a spring, and a front view

## The idea

The curves in the book are drawn as a graph: input along the bottom, output up the side.
That graph is a flat picture of something rounder. Say so, in one plain sentence near the
zigzag figure:

> This graph is the side view of a spring. Look at the spring from the front, along its axis,
> and you see something else: a circle, for a sine, and a diamond, for the tent.

Then add a diagram of the spring from the front.

## What is true (checked by hand, 15 Nov 2026; to be checked again in the notebook)

- **The sine is the side view of a helix.** A point that moves along an axis at constant
  speed while it goes round a circle traces a helix. Seen from the side, its height is
  `sin(frequency * input)`. Seen from the front, along the axis, it is the circle. The
  front view shows the information the side view hides: the second coordinate, the cosine.
  Together `(cos, sin)` is the full state, and the graph is only one shadow of it. The same
  picture is the complex exponential `e^(i * frequency * input)`, a helix, which is the
  natural lead-in to the Fourier subsection of chapter 7 (a real sine is the shadow of that
  helix).
- **The tent zigzag is the side view of a spring with a square winding.** Let `x(t)` be the
  triangle wave of period 4 that goes 1, 0, -1, 0, 1 at `t = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4` in straight
  pieces, and let `y(t) = x(t - 1)`. The point `(x, y)` runs from `(1, 0)` to `(0, 1)` to
  `(-1, 0)` to `(0, -1)` in straight lines at constant speed: a diamond (a square turned by
  45 degrees), where `|x| + |y| = 1`. So the zigzag is the shadow of a point going round a
  diamond while it moves along the axis. The front view is the diamond.
- **Folding is squeezing a spring.** The same length of axis holds more coils when each is
  narrower. Each tent layer doubles the number of coils (the pieces double), so after
  `layer_count` layers the spring has `2 ^ layer_count` half-coils in the same length. This is
  the picture of chapter 7's squeeze, `model(squeeze_factor * input)`: the squeeze factor is the
  number of coils per unit length, the coils get closer together, and the amplitude (the radius
  of the spring) does not change. That is why the vertical error does not improve.
- **The polarizer chapter already uses the picture.** `docs/networks/three-polarizers.md`
  says the photon's state traces a quarter turn of a helix around the beam when the plates
  are smoothed out, and its vibration is the arrow whose tip goes round. Link the two.

## Depth and width in the spring picture (the reading proposed on 15 Nov 2026)

The reading proposed: "depth is the reach of the spring, width is its resolution". Part of
it holds and part does not. Say which, in the chapter, rather than leave the reader to guess.

**Where it connects.**
- Depth does set something about the spring's geometry: with the chosen tent weights, each
  layer doubles the coils per unit length, so `layer_count` layers give `2 ^ layer_count`
  half-coils. That is the squeeze factor of chapter 7.
- "Resolution" is a fair word for that: more coils per unit length means finer detail along
  the axis. So the intuition (one of depth or width controls fineness) is sound.
- Width also adds coils: a single ReLU layer with `n` units gives about `n + 1` pieces.

**Where it does not.**
- **Depth is not the reach.** The reach would be the length of axis the spring covers, which
  is the input range. Neither depth nor width sets it; a network is defined on every input.
  What depth sets is the winding (coils per unit length), which is closer to resolution.
- **Width is not the resolution.** Width adds coils only linearly (`n + 1`), where depth
  adds them exponentially (`2 ^ layer_count`). So in the tent picture it is depth that gives
  resolution, and width a slow additive version of the same thing.
- **The radius is set by neither.** The amplitude does not change with layers; that is why
  the vertical error does not improve (chapter 7).
- **Only for the chosen tent weights.** With random weights nothing here is proved: more
  width does not lengthen the curve, and depth's effect turns on the weight scale (no growth
  from 1 to 16 layers at the standard scale; about ninefold at 1.5 times the variance). In
  the spring picture: the weight scale decides whether depth winds the spring or only bends it.

# TODO: chapter 18, an eigen version of "spectral bias" instead of the sine version

Opened 17 Nov 2026. Raised on reading "spectral": it suggests an eigendecomposition, not
sines.

Page: https://ghost---shadow.github.io/squiggly-line-theorem/18-strong-directions-first.html
Source: [book/18-strong-directions-first.md](book/18-strong-directions-first.md), lean
[SpectralBias.lean](lean/SquigglyLine/Supporting/Training/SpectralBias.lean), note
[docs/supporting/training/spectral-bias.md](docs/supporting/training/spectral-bias.md).

## The question

Is there a version of chapter 18 that is stated with the eigendecomposition of the kernel
rather than with a sine (Fourier) reading? Find out whether it is possible, and what it adds.

## What is already true (read from the chapter, 17 Nov 2026)

- **The proved statement is already an eigen statement.** `spectral_bias` takes a `direction`
  with `predict (adjoint predict direction) = kernel_strength • direction`. That says
  `direction` is an eigenvector of the kernel `K = predict ∘ adjoint predict`, with
  eigenvalue `kernel_strength`. The theorem is about one eigenvector at a time. So the
  Lean is not the sine version; the sine appears only in the prose.
- **The sine enters only through one sentence.** "In the Fourier model the strength of a
  frequency is the square of the size its coefficient is allowed" (the section "How this
  meets the squiggly line"), and the claim that the strong directions are the smooth ones,
  which the chapter marks **open** (twice).
- **What is not yet in the chapter.** The whole error is never split into eigen-directions.
  The chapter treats one direction, or compares two. The spectral theorem (a symmetric
  positive semidefinite `K` has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors) is what turns
  "each direction has its own clock" into a statement about the whole loss.

## What an eigen version would say

Take `K` with orthonormal eigenvectors `v_1, ..., v_m` and eigenvalues `lambda_1 >= ... >=
lambda_m >= 0`. Write the starting error as `r_0 = sum c_i v_i`. After `t` gradient steps
(learning rate `eta`, with `eta * lambda_i <= 1`):

- the error is `r_t = sum (1 - eta * lambda_i)^t * c_i * v_i`;
- the squared loss is `sum (1 - eta * lambda_i)^(2t) * c_i^2`, a sum of one decaying term
  per eigen-direction, each with its own clock `1 / (eta * lambda_i)`;
- "strong directions first" becomes: the top eigenvectors of `K` are learned first.

This needs no sines, no smoothness and no Fourier model. It holds for any kernel.

## Where the sine version fits

- **Fourier is a special case.** If the data are evenly spaced and the kernel only depends on
  the gap between inputs (the Fourier lift has kernel `cos(w (x - x'))`, chapter 11), then
  `K` is circulant, and its eigenvectors are the sines and cosines (the discrete Fourier
  basis). The eigenvalues are the squared coefficient sizes. So the sine version is the
  eigen version for a shift-invariant kernel, and the chapter's sentence follows from it.
- **That is where "smooth means strong" lives.** It is a claim about which eigenvalues are
  large, which depends on the kernel and the data. For the Fourier model it is a choice of
  coefficient sizes, not a derived fact. In the general eigen version it is a statement
  about the spectrum of `K`, and is a separate question.

## What to do

1. **Write the eigen statement.** Prove, from the existing lemmas
   (`inner_residual_iterate_gradientStep` and the spectral theorem for a self-adjoint
   operator in Mathlib), that the error after `t` steps is the sum above and the loss is the
   sum of squares of the decayed coefficients. Check what Mathlib provides
   (`LinearMap.IsSymmetric.eigenvectorBasis` and the `hasEigenvalues` results) and what must be
   built. Keep to finite dimensions.
2. **State the order of learning on the whole loss.** The share of the loss still in
   direction `i` after `t` steps is `(1 - eta * lambda_i)^(2t)`; the loss decomposes into
   these shares weighted by `c_i^2`. A monotone statement: a larger eigenvalue leaves less.
3. **Prove the Fourier spectrum version.** This is the eigen statement applied to the
   Fourier model, and it should exist as its own theorem, not a remark. Setting: `N` evenly
   spaced inputs over one period, and the Fourier model with a coefficient-size `a_k` at
   each frequency `w_k`. Its kernel is `K(x, x') = sum_k a_k^2 cos(w_k (x - x'))`, which
   depends only on the gap, so the kernel matrix is circulant. Then:
   - the eigenvectors of `K` are the sampled cosines and sines at the frequencies `w_k`;
   - the eigenvalue of frequency `k` is about `(N / 2) * a_k^2` (the constant term gets
     `N * a_0^2`); the exact factor is to be derived and checked, not assumed;
   - so the error at frequency `k` shrinks by `1 - eta * (N / 2) * a_k^2` each step, and its
     clock is `1 / (eta * (N / 2) * a_k^2)`;
   - the statement the chapter now only asserts follows: if the high frequencies have small
     `a_k`, they have long clocks and are learned last. That is "smooth first", proved for
     this model, with the coefficient sizes as the stated hypothesis. It is still not a claim
     about a real network (see the open items below).
   If the circulant eigenvector fact is not in Mathlib, prove it for the discrete Fourier
   basis directly (a sampled `exp(2 pi i j k / N)` is an eigenvector of a circulant), or take
   the Fourier modes as a hypothesis and say which.
   Conditions to state: evenly spaced data over whole periods; the frequencies are the
   multiples of the base frequency; below the Nyquist frequency (frequencies above `N / 2`
   alias onto lower ones, which is itself a result worth a line). For irregular data the
   Fourier modes are no longer the eigenvectors, which is the reason for the general
   eigen version of steps 1 and 2.
4. **Run a numerical experiment alongside** (the book's rule): build a kernel matrix for
   evenly spaced data and for irregular data, train, compare the error in each eigen-direction
   against the formula, and compare with the Fourier version. For irregular data the sines
   should stop being the right directions and the eigenvectors should still be; that is the
   point of the eigen version. Each check must be able to fail, with a control where the

5. **Check the connection to the open item.** The chapter's "that smooth means strong" claim
   asks which directions are strong in a real network. In the eigen version this is the
   spectrum of the neural tangent kernel, a known object (it is the setting of Rahaman and of
   the tangent-kernel literature). Record it, do not claim it. It is a **recalled** claim, so

# TODO: always say why a lower and an upper bound hold, next to the bound

Opened 17 Nov 2026. Raised on reading `spectral_bias` in chapter 18.

## The rule to add

Wherever a statement or proof gives a **lower bound** or an **upper bound**, put a short
comment in plain words saying **why that bound is true**, in the Lean file and in the book,
next to the line. A bound with no "why" is a number the reader has to trust. The "why" is
usually one sentence and one picture, and it is worth more than the bound.

The model is the pair in chapter 18 (`spectral_bias`):

```
let lower_bound := (1 - step_count * rate) * |start_error|
let upper_bound := Real.exp (-(step_count * rate)) * |start_error|
```

The exact share left is `(1 - rate) ^ step_count`, with `0 <= rate <= 1`. The two bounds
sandwich it:

- **Lower bound, `1 - step_count * rate` (Bernoulli's inequality).** Each step removes
  `rate` of what is left. If every step removed `rate` of the *original* error, `step_count`
  steps would remove `step_count * rate` in all. But what is left keeps shrinking, so each
  step removes less than that. So you remove no more than `step_count * rate`, and at least
  `1 - step_count * rate` is left. It only says something while `step_count * rate < 1`,
  that is, before about one clock has run (the "no direction can be hurried" half).
- **Upper bound, `exp(-(step_count * rate))`.** `1 - rate <= exp(-rate)`: the curve
  `exp(-x)` lies above its tangent line `1 - x` at 0, because it is convex. Raise both sides
  to the power `step_count` (allowed because `1 - rate >= 0`) and `(1 - rate)^step_count <=
  exp(-step_count * rate)`. In words: stepping by a fraction each time never beats smooth
  decay at the same rate (the "every direction gets learned in the end" half).
- **How tight they are.** Chapter 18's example, rate `0.005` and `20` steps: lower `0.9`,
  exact about `0.9046`, upper about `0.9048`. For a strong direction (rate `0.5`, `5`
  steps) the exact share is about `0.031`, the upper bound `0.082`, and the lower bound is
  negative and says nothing.

## Where to put it

- **Lean.** A comment above each `lower_bound` / `upper_bound` line, and a one-line "why"
  above each named bound lemma (`one_sub_mul_le_pow_one_sub`, `pow_one_sub_le_exp`,
  `pow_one_sub_anti`), as [lean/STYLE.md](lean/STYLE.md) already asks for steps that carry
  the idea of the proof. Add a line to the "Comments" section of `lean/STYLE.md` and to
  `lean/PORTING.md`: every bound states why it holds, in one sentence, and which direction
  of the sandwich it is.
- **Book.** In the "Why it is true" section of each chapter, give each bound its own short
  paragraph with the picture (as above), not only the lemma name. Also in `book/WRITING.md`,
  next to the rule that names say what a thing is.
- **Helper agents.** Put the rule in the shared brief on disk (`lean/PORTING.md`,
  `book/WRITING.md`) once, so each agent gets only its delta.

## Check how central it is

The claim "this is the linchpin of all the theorems" has not been checked. A grep on

## The question

Is there a way to guess the hyperparameters of a ReLU dense network or a ReLU transformer
analytically, from the theory, before training? The author's view: it would be genuinely
helpful if the theory can predict them. If it cannot yet, that is fine: **put it on the
wishlist** (`docs/wishlist.md`), with what exists and what is in the way. The task below
is to find out which, and to do the wishlist step if the attempt fails.

## What we have so far (all for a model linear in its weights, with a fixed kernel)

From chapters 18 and 19 (read 17 Nov 2026), proved in Lean for the fixed-kernel case:

- **The largest stable learning rate.** A direction of kernel strength `s` is stable exactly
  when `learning_rate < 2 / s` (`stable_iff_lt_two_div`). So the whole model is stable exactly
  when `learning_rate < 2 / s_max`, where `s_max` is the largest eigenvalue of the kernel.
- **The best step.** If the strengths lie between `weakest` and `strongest`, the step
  `2 / (weakest + strongest)` gives every direction a factor of size at most
  `(strongest - weakest) / (strongest + weakest)` (`abs_one_sub_best_step_le`), and the
  weakest direction cannot be promised more.
- **How many steps.** Each direction has a clock of about `1 / (learning_rate * strength)`
  steps (chapter 18), with the two bounds, so the number of steps to learn a direction can be
  read off its strength.
- **Momentum.** The edge moves to `learning_rate * strength = 2 * (1 + momentum)` for the
  alternating error only; stability below it is not shown.
- **Rescaling.** The note on flat minima: what matters is `learning_rate * strength`, which
  rescaling the weights leaves alone. A squigglier model has stronger directions, so for a
  fixed learning rate it sits nearer the edge.

So for a *linear* model the learning rate and the step count are predicted exactly by the
kernel's eigenvalues. Nothing here yet says what the kernel of a ReLU network *is*.

## What is missing for a ReLU dense network or a transformer (from the chapter and the wishlist)

- **The kernel moves.** Chapter 19 "What is not shown": all of it is for a fixed kernel.
  The wishlist lists "a wide network trains like a model linear in its weights" (Jacot et al.
  2018, Lee et al. 2019) under *out of reach: the mathematics is known*. That result is what
  would carry the learning-rate formula to wide ReLU networks, and it is not formalised here.
- **The sharpness climbs and sits at `2 / learning_rate`** (Cohen et al. 2021, recalled).
  The wishlist lists why as *nobody knows yet*. It means the initial kernel can understate
  the sharpness the network reaches, so `2 / s_max` at initialisation may be too large a
  guess.
- **Adam.** The wishlist lists "why Adam beats plain gradient descent on transformers" as
  *nobody knows yet*. The chapter's edge is for plain gradient descent only.
- **Width, depth and initial scale.** "How to scale the weights and step sizes with width"
  (maximal update parametrisation, Yang and Hu 2021) is on the wishlist as *out of reach:
  the mathematics is known*. The random-network squiggliness formula is *nobody knows yet*.
- **Transformers.** Attention is an average and cannot create folds; the folds come from the
  feed-forward block (wishlist, "Averages"). There is no kernel statement for softmax
  attention here.

Opened 17 Nov 2026. A suggestion from the author, following the hyperparameter question above
(chapter 19).

## The idea

Put a small calculator in the chapter page: the reader types in the numbers of a model and
gets the learning rate, step count and so on that the chapter's theorems give, with the decay
curves drawn live. It turns "the largest stable step is `2 / strength`" from a sentence into
something to try.

## Two tiers, because only one is proved

**Tier 1: the exact calculator, for a model linear in its weights with a fixed kernel.**
Everything it computes is a theorem already in the book. Build this first.

Inputs: the kernel's strengths (strongest and weakest, or a short list), the learning rate,
optionally the momentum, the starting error, and a tolerance.

Outputs, each labelled with the theorem it comes from:
- the **edge**, `2 / strongest` (`stable_iff_lt_two_div`), and whether the chosen learning
  rate is stable, at the edge, or past it (`shrinks_below_edge`, `holds_at_edge`,
  `grows_past_edge`);
- the **best step**, `2 / (weakest + strongest)`, and the guaranteed speed
  `(strongest - weakest) / (strongest + weakest)` (`abs_one_sub_best_step_le`);
- for each direction, the **factor** `1 - learning_rate * strength` and the **clock**
  `1 / (learning_rate * strength)` steps (chapter 18);
- the **steps to reach the tolerance**: exactly, from `(1 - rate)^t`, and with the two bounds
  (`1 - t * rate` and `exp(-t * rate)`), so the reader sees how loose each is;
- the momentum edge `2 * (1 + momentum)`, with the note that it is proved for the alternating
  error only.

A plot beside it: share of error left against steps, one line per direction, with the bounds
as a band, redrawn as the reader changes a number. This is the figure planned for chapter 18
(`18-eigen-decay`), made live.

**Tier 2: a guess for a ReLU dense network or transformer.** Only if the experiments in the
section above succeed by their decision rule. It would estimate `lambda_max` of the tangent
kernel at initialisation and print `2 / lambda_max` as a guess. It must say, in the output
itself, that this is a **measured** rule with the measured spread, not a theorem. If the
experiments fail and the item goes on the wishlist, Tier 2 is not built; Tier 1 still stands.

## Where it goes

- **Chapter 19** (the largest step), after "The statement": the edge, the best step, the
  stable / edge / unstable verdict.
- **Chapter 18** (strong directions first): the clocks and the two bounds, on the plot.
- Later, if it works: chapters 20 to 23 (weight decay, early stopping, batch size, Adam) get
  their own small calculators, only where the chapter has an exact statement.

## What has to be made visible

Four ideas, in the order a reader meets them:

1. **The kernel.** A table, one entry per pair of data points, of how much the model treats
   them as alike (the model's matrix times its own transpose).
2. **Kernel strength.** How much the kernel stretches the error along one direction (an
   eigenvalue). A strong direction moves easily; a weak one barely moves.
3. **The factor.** `1 - learning_rate * strength`: what is left of the error along that
   direction after one step.
4. **The edge.** The learning rate `2 / strength` where the size of the factor reaches 1.
   Below it the error shrinks; above it the error grows.

And one consequence: **the sharpest direction sets the largest step for the whole model**, and
a wide spread of strengths makes the weakest direction crawl.

# TODO: visual guides and experimental justification for "the direction is stretched" (and the claims I made about it)

Opened 17 Nov 2026. The author cast doubt on the explanation of this line of
`spectral_bias` and `stable_iff_lt_two_div` (chapters 18 and 19):

```
(direction_is_stretched :
  predict (adjoint predict direction) = kernel_strength • direction)
```

and asked for visual guides and experimental justification, not words alone.

## What was claimed (and so what is doubted)

The explanation given in chat made these claims. Each one is to be shown, not asserted:

1. The line says `direction` is an **eigenvector** of the kernel `K = A Aᵀ`, with eigenvalue
   `kernel_strength`: `K d = λ d`. (A statement about what the Lean says.)
2. For such a direction the kernel **only stretches it, and does not turn it**.
3. If the error points along such a direction, one gradient step multiplies it by exactly
   `1 - learning_rate * strength`, with **no leakage into other directions**.
4. For a direction that is *not* stretched, there is **no single factor**; the error mixes.
5. The condition is **not rare**: `K` is symmetric, so there is a full set of such directions
   and **any error splits into them**, each evolving on its own.
6. In a real network the kernel moves, so such a direction **stops being stretched** during
   training (this is why the chapter calls the deep case open).

Doubt is the right response to 5 and 6 especially: 5 is a general theorem about matrices
quoted from memory, and 6 is an expectation, not a result.

## What would settle it: experiments (each must be able to fail)

A notebook `experiments/part4/ch19_the_largest_step.ipynb` (the same one as the figure section
above), seeded, following [experiments/WRITING.md](experiments/WRITING.md), results **measured**.

1. **The hypothesis can hold (non-vacuity).** Build a small linear model (`A`, say 6 data
   points, 4 weights, or the Fourier model). Compute `K = A Aᵀ` and its eigenpairs. Assert
   `K d = λ d` for each eigenpair to numerical tolerance (residual around 1e-12). **Control
   that must fail:** a random vector that is not an eigenvector gives a large residual.
   This also answers the "look for an instance" check in [DISCLAIMER.md](DISCLAIMER.md): it
   shows the hypothesis is satisfiable.
2. **One factor, exactly, for a stretched direction.** Start the error along an eigenvector,
   run real gradient descent on the model (not the formula), and assert the error equals
   `(1 - learning_rate * strength)^t * start` at every step, and that it stays along the same
   direction (the component off the direction stays at 0 to tolerance).
3. **No single factor for a non-stretched direction (the control for claim 4).** Start the error
   along a coordinate axis (not an eigenvector). Assert the error *leaves* that axis, and that
   the component along it is **not** a single geometric sequence (fit a line to the log of its
   size; the residual of the fit must be large). If this control passes as a single factor, the
   hypothesis would be unnecessary and the doubt would be right; the experiment must be able
   to show that.
4. **Any error splits (claim 5).** Draw a random start error. Project it onto the eigenvectors
   and assert: (a) the pieces add back to the error, (b) each piece follows its own
   `(1 - learning_rate * strength_i)^t`, (c) the sum of the pieces equals the real error at
   every step. Repeat over seeds and over models of several shapes, including a wide one (more
   weights than data) and a narrow one (fewer weights than data, where the kernel has zero
   strengths and some directions never move, as the chapter says).
5. **Awkward cases.** Repeated strengths (a whole plane of stretched directions, any vector in
   it works), strength zero (never moves), strengths spread over orders of magnitude.
6. **Outside the model: the kernel moves (claim 6).** A small ReLU network trained by gradient
   descent. Take the top few eigenvectors of its tangent kernel at the start. At each step
   recompute the kernel and measure how far each starting eigenvector is from being stretched:
   `|| K_t d - (d^T K_t d) d || / || K_t d ||`, and the cosine with the nearest current
   eigenvector. Do it at several widths and seeds. Expected: the number stays near 0 for a wide
   network and grows for a narrow one. If it stays near 0 for all, the deep case may be
   easier than the chapter says; if it grows quickly for all, the gap is real. Either is a
   result, and goes in "What is not shown".

# TODO: units of measurement and dimensional analysis for the repository ("50 what?")

Opened 17 Nov 2026. Raised on reading the first "Check yourself" question of chapter 19:
"A direction has strength 50. What is the largest stable learning rate?" The author asked
"50 what?" and suggested adding units, with dimensional analysis in the notation of physics
(like `[M L T^-2]` for a force). This is the assessment and the plan.

Source: [book/19-the-largest-step.md](book/19-the-largest-step.md); the rescaling result is
[Reparam.lean](lean/SquigglyLine/Supporting/Training/Reparam.lean) and the flat-minima note.

## The verdict: it makes sense, in a limited way

Machine learning has no mass, length or time, so we invent base dimensions for the things that
are actually different kinds of quantity:

| Symbol | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `[Y]` | the units of an output (a label, a prediction, an error) | metres, dollars, a probability |
| `[W]` | the units of a weight | what a unit of weight does to the output |
| `[X]` | the units of an input feature | seconds, pixels |
| steps | dimensionless: a count | `t`, `step_count` |

(Many chapters use data scaled to `[-1, 1]`, where these are all dimensionless. That is a
choice; dimensional analysis is the discipline of keeping the units explicit so the formulas
can be checked.)

For the linear model `output = A * weights`, with squared loss, the dimensions follow:

| Quantity | Dimension | Why |
|---|---|---|
| the matrix `A` (the model) | `[Y]/[W]` | takes weights to outputs |
| the error (residual) | `[Y]` | an output minus a label |
| the loss `0.5 * error^2` | `[Y]^2` | |
| the gradient `A^T * error` | `[Y]^2/[W]` | `[A]` times `[error]` |
| the learning rate | `[W]^2/[Y]^2` | the step `learning_rate * gradient` must be a weight: `[W]` |
| the kernel `K = A A^T`, and so its strength (eigenvalue) | `[Y]^2/[W]^2` | `[A]^2` |
| `learning_rate * strength` | **dimensionless** | `([W]^2/[Y]^2) * ([Y]^2/[W]^2)` |
| the edge `2 / strength` | `[W]^2/[Y]^2` | the same as a learning rate, as it must be |
| the factor `1 - learning_rate * strength` | dimensionless | so it can be compared with 1 |

So "strength 50" has units. Example: outputs in metres, features in metres, and a dimensionless
weight, so `[Y] = m` and `[W] = 1`. Then the strength is `50 m^2`, and the largest stable
learning rate is `2 / 50 = 0.04 per m^2`. The chapter's number is right; its unit was missing.

## What dimensional analysis buys

1. **It explains why the edge is `2 / strength` and nothing else.** The only dimensionless
   group built from the learning rate and the kernel is `learning_rate * strength`. Stability
   can only depend on that product, so the threshold has to be a pure number divided by the
   strength. The proof says it is 2; the dimensions say its form.
2. **It is the rescaling result in another dress.** The flat-minima note says that rescaling
   the weights by `c` divides the step size by `c^2`. With `[learning_rate] = [W]^2/[Y]^2`, a
   change of weight unit by `c` changes the learning rate's number by `c^2`, and
   `learning_rate * strength` is left alone, as chapter 19's "How this meets the squiggly line"
   says. That is a dimensional consequence, so units make `Reparam.lean` obvious and extend it.
3. **It is a cheap check on every formula.** The two sides of an equation, and every term of a
   sum, must have the same dimensions. A bound like `1 - step_count * rate` is consistent only if
   `rate` is dimensionless, which it is. A formula that fails this has a mistake (the sign
   error in the old derivation was of a different kind, but this class of slip is common).
4. **It gives a reason for some conventions.** `tanh`, `exp`, softmax and cross-entropy need a
   dimensionless argument, so their inputs must be dimensionless or carry a scale (a
   temperature, `1/sqrt(head_dim)` in attention). ReLU is homogeneous, so units pass through
   it. A cross-entropy loss is dimensionless, so then `[learning_rate] = [W]^2` with no
   `[Y]`. (These are **argued** from the dimensions; test before stating them in a chapter.)
5. **A conjecture to test, not a claim:** in a network with several layers, each layer's weights
   carry their own unit (the hidden units are a free scale, because ReLU is positively
   homogeneous). A single learning rate for all layers is then dimensionally inconsistent unless
   the hidden unit is fixed, which would be a dimensional reading of why per-layer learning rates
   and width-dependent scaling (maximal update parametrisation, on the wishlist) are natural.
   Mark **argued** at most until checked.

## Where it does not apply, or is cheap

- Where data is normalised, everything is dimensionless and the analysis says nothing new.
  Say so, and do not pretend otherwise.
- Lean and Mathlib are not set up for physical units. I do not know of a dimension-typed
  quantity library in Mathlib that fits; check before assuming. Enforcing units in the Lean
  types would be heavy and is not proposed.
- A dimension has no force on a proof: it cannot make a false theorem true. It is a check and
  an explanation, not a method of proof.

## Plan

1. **Write the dimension table** above into a short glossary entry ("dimension of a quantity"),
   and a "Units" line near the top of chapters 10, 18 and 19, where the quantities are
   introduced. Do not explain dimensional analysis at length: it is taught in school physics,
   so say it once, plainly (see the audience rule above).
2. **Fix the "Check yourself" question and its answer.** Question 1 of chapter 19 becomes, for
   example, "outputs in metres, features in metres, a dimensionless weight; a direction has
   strength `50 m^2`. What is the largest stable learning rate?" Answer: `2 / 50 = 0.04 per m^2`.
   Do the same for other numeric examples that give a strength or a learning rate.
3. **A dimensional pass over the formulas of chapters 10 and 18 to 23.** For each statement,
   write the dimension of each symbol and check both sides. Record any formula that mixes
   dimensions. Do not change a statement or a label; report mismatches and decide each.
4. **A notebook check that can fail** (in the chapter 19 notebook): rescale the weights by `c`
   and the outputs by `s`, recompute the kernel strength and the learning rate that sits at the
   edge, and assert they transform by exactly `s^2/c^2` and `c^2/s^2`, with `learning_rate *
   strength` unchanged. Control that must fail: scaling the strength by `c` instead of `s^2/c^2`
   must break the assertion. Also check that the measured divergence threshold of real gradient
   descent follows the same scaling.
5. **Lean, only if cheap:** `Reparam.lean` already has the weights half. Its header defines
   `RESCALE` as "the model with its parameters in other units" and proves `rescale_smul` and

6. **Test the multi-layer conjecture** (item 5 above) with a small ReLU network: scale the
   hidden units by `c` in one layer and `1/c` in the next (the output is unchanged), and measure

   predicted `c^2`, say it is **measured**. If not, say so and drop the claim.

## How BibTeX works with Markdown (the question)

Markdown has no citations of its own, and GitHub does not render any. **Pandoc** adds them: you
write `[@belkin2019reconciling]` in the text, give pandoc `--citeproc --bibliography references.bib`
(and optionally a style file, `--csl`), and it replaces each mark with an in-text citation and
puts a reference list at the end. For a PDF it can also hand the keys to LaTeX (`--natbib` or
`--biblatex`) instead. So it works for the PDF and the site, not for the chapter as shown on
GitHub, where the reader would see the raw `[@belkin2019reconciling]`.

Whether pandoc's `gfm` reader accepts `[@key]` marks, or needs `+citations` (the book builds with
`--from gfm`), is **not known**; it must be tested where pandoc is installed.

## The standing rule (the author, 17 Nov 2026)

**All theorems must have an explanatory diagram and graphs which show experimental results.**

Two things for each theorem the book states:
1. **An explanatory diagram**: a picture of what the statement says (the objects, what is held
   fixed, what changes), so that a reader can see the claim before reading the symbols. A
   schematic is allowed if it is labelled as one and uses the real numbers of the example.
2. **A graph of experimental results**: a measured (or exactly computed, and labelled so) plot
   from the chapter's notebook that shows the claim holding, with the bound or exact value on
   the same axes, and a control where it should fail or be loose.

This sharpens the earlier item in this proposal ("most chapters have no figure"). That item
asked for at least one figure per chapter from 14 to 46; this rule asks for a diagram and a
graph per theorem, with no exceptions unless stated.

## Why they belong

Both are the standard baselines of machine unlearning, the research on making a trained model
behave as if it had not seen some data (**recalled**: they appear as the simple baselines in most
unlearning papers and benchmarks, for example as "gradient ascent", "fine-tuning" and "gradient
difference"; names and years not checked). The wishlist keeps to established, commonly used
ideas, and these are the most common ones. They are baselines, not the best methods, and that is
the reason to take them first: the simplest case is the one the theory can reach.

Both connect to the squiggly line through results already in the repo, so neither is standalone:

- **Forgetting by a gradient step** is already proved for a model that is linear in its weights.
  A step on one task changes the errors on another by the step size times the cross kernel (the
  table of likeness between the two sets of inputs) times the first task's errors
  (`residual_gradientStep_other` and `inner_forgetting_step`, in
  `lean/SquigglyLine/Supporting/NewInputs/Forgetting.lean`; notes in
  `docs/supporting/new-inputs/forgetting.md`).
- **The one-factor law** of chapter 13: after `step_count` steps the residual is
  `(1 - learning_rate * squared_length) ^ step_count` times the start, for tight features.
- **Pruning and forgetting** is chapter 45, and weight decay and early stopping is chapter 20.
