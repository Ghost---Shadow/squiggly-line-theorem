## What counts as a result here

- A strategy that a practitioner knows by name and that is in common use.
- One plain sentence that says why it works, in this repository's terms (slope, nearness,
  coverage, averages, windows, the bottleneck), and one that says what it gives up.
- A Lean theorem for the "why", about a small model that the text names: one attention
  head, a stack of windows, one memory cell. Plain English first, then Lean.
- A paragraph that says what the theorem does **not** say. These are statements about one
  piece of a model at a time. None of them says how a real model behaves at a million tokens.

## Marks used below

- **proved**: a theorem in `lean/SquigglyLine/` with that name exists today.
- **to prove**: a statement I believe is true and short to prove. Not in Lean.
- **checked by hand**: derived on paper and checked with numbers on 25 Nov 2026. Not in Lean.
- **my reading**: an interpretation of mine. Not derived.
- **recalled**: every name of a method or paper in this file is from memory and is not
  checked against a source. Check each one before it is written anywhere else.

## A. Positions

### A1. Relative positions

- **Why it works.** The score between two tokens depends only on how far apart they are.
  A pattern learned near the start of the context is the same pattern anywhere else.
- **Proved.** `rotary_score_depends_on_position_gap`,
  `relative_bias_does_not_change_under_a_shift`,
  `position_overlap_does_not_change_under_a_shift` in `Networks/Positions.lean`. The
  contrast is also proved: `learned_table_ties_no_two_positions` says a learned table of
  positions ties no two positions together.
- **To prove.** That a whole attention layer with such scores gives the same outputs when
  every position is shifted by the same amount.
- **What it gives up.** Gaps longer than any seen in training are still new inputs. A3 is
  the usual answer.

### A2. A penalty on distance

- **Why it works.** Each step of distance takes a fixed amount `m` off the score. With the
  other part of the score bounded by `B`, the tokens further than `D` steps away get, all
  together, a share of at most `exp(2B) × exp(-m(D + 1)) / (1 - exp(-m))` (**to prove**;
  **checked by hand**). So the output with a long context is close to the output with only
  the nearest `D` tokens, and a model trained on short contexts sees almost the same thing
  on long ones.
- **Proved.** The pieces: `relative_bias_does_not_change_under_a_shift`,
  `two_weighted_averages_differ_by_at_most_the_spread` in
  `Supporting/NetworkShape/AttentionRate.lean`.
- **What it gives up.** The same bound says the head cannot read far tokens. The method
  trades reach for stability. Dropping the oldest tokens from a context is the same trade,
  made by hand.

### A3. Stretching rotary positions

- **Why it works.** Multiply every position by `trained length / new length`. Then every
  gap of the longer context is a gap that was seen in training (**to prove**; it is one
  line).
- **The price.** This is the squeeze of chapter 7. Neighbouring tokens are now closer
  together in phase by the same factor, and telling them apart takes that much more slope
  (**proved** in general: `squeezing_multiplies_the_slope_limit` in
  `Theorem/Compression.lean`; the statement for position codes is **to prove**).
- **Why the later methods squeeze only the slow clocks.** A frequency `ω` has gone once
  round its circle within the trained length only if `ω × trained length` is at least `2π`.
  A fast clock has shown the model every phase already and needs no squeeze. A slow one has
  not (**my reading**; **to prove** as a coverage statement with `COVERS_WITHIN`).
- **What it gives up.** Resolution between neighbours for the clocks that are squeezed.

## B. Attending to less

### B1. Sliding window, local attention

- **Why it works.** One layer reads a window. A stack of `layer_count` layers reads
  `layer_count × (window - 1)` tokens to each side, and exactly that many.
- **Proved.** `convolution_stack_reads_only_its_exact_window` in
  `Supporting/NetworkShape/Convolution.lean`.
- **What it gives up.** Nothing beyond that reach can change the output. The same theorem
  says so.

### B2. Windows that widen with depth

- **Why it works.** If each layer reads tokens twice as far apart as the layer below, the
  reach is about `2 ^ layer_count`, the same doubling as the tent layers of chapter 8.
- **Proved.** The pieces on stride: `stride_is_squeezing`, `stride_doubles_the_slope`.
- **To prove.** The reach of a stack with doubling gaps.
- **What it gives up.** Slope: `stride_doubles_the_slope` is the price of each doubling.

### B3. Global tokens

- **Why it works.** One token reads every token and is read by every token. Any two tokens
  are then two layers apart, whatever the length (**to prove**).
- **What it gives up.** The hub hands on an average of everything
  (`attention_output_lies_among_the_values`, **proved**), and a hub of fixed size is a
  bottleneck (`bottleneck`, **proved**).

### B4. Keeping only the top scores

- **Why it works.** If the dropped tokens had shares that add to `p`, the head's output
  moves by at most `p` times the range of the values (**to prove**; **checked by hand**).
  At zero temperature only the top score counts anyway (`cold_softmax_picks_the_top_score`,
  **proved**).
- **What it gives up.** With bounded scores and many tokens, the small shares add up:
  `every_token_gets_a_share` (**proved**) gives each at least `exp(-2B) / n`, so `p` is not
  small unless the scores are spread out.

### B5. Random features for attention

- **Why it works.** For keys of one length, the attention weight is a bump kernel of the
  distance between query and key (`softmax_of_overlap_equals_softmax_of_distance`,
  **proved**). A bump kernel is the average of random cosines
  (`random_fourier_kernel_is_the_bump_kernel` in `Networks/RandomFourier.lean`, **proved**).
  So the weights can be made from a fixed number of random waves, without comparing every
  pair of tokens.
- **To prove.** The sentence that joins the two.
- **What it gives up.** An average of finitely many random waves is only near the kernel.
  How near is not proved here.
- **Note.** This is known by name but is less used in large models than the others
  (**my reading**). Lowest priority.

## C. Not computing twice

### C1. The key-value cache, prompt caching

- **Why it works.** With a causal mask, the output at a position does not change when
  tokens are added after it. So what was computed for the earlier tokens can be stored and
  used again, layer after layer (**to prove**, by induction over the layers).
- **What it gives up.** Nothing in accuracy. It costs memory, which is why D3 exists.
- **Note.** This is the most used strategy on the list and has no theorem in the library
  yet. Do it first.

## D. Putting less into the context

### D1. Retrieval

- **Why it works.** Finding the nearest passages by their embeddings is what an attention
  head does at zero temperature, over the whole corpus, before the model runs.
- **Proved.** `softmax_of_cosine_equals_softmax_of_distance_for_unit_vectors` and
  `cold_softmax_picks_the_top_score`; `nearest_neighbour_error_is_at_most_slope_times_distance`
  in `Supporting/NewInputs/Neighbours.lean`.
- **To prove.** That reading only the retrieved passages differs from reading everything by
  at most the dropped share times the range (the bound of B4, applied to passages).
- **What it gives up.** Whatever the retriever scored low. Two passages scored the same are
  blended (`equal_scores_give_the_point_half_way`, **proved**).

### D2. Chunk and summarise

- **Why it works.** If the answer depends on the context only through something the summary
  keeps, the summary loses nothing (**to prove**; it is a statement about a function that
  factors through another). Averaging the answers of several chunks is an ensemble
  (`ensemble_error_is_at_most_average_member_error`, **proved**).
- **What it gives up.** A summary of fixed size must give two different contexts the same
  summary once there are more contexts than summaries
  (`memorising_needs_one_bit_per_stored_bit` and `bottleneck`, **proved**; the statement for
  a sequence of tokens is **to prove**). Compaction and written notes are the same case.

### D3. A coarser cache

- **Why it works.** Snapping a stored token to the nearest entry of a table moves the
  output by at most slope times the distance moved
  (`snapping_moves_output_at_most_slope_times_distance`, `nearest_entry_is_the_gentlest_snap`
  in `Supporting/NetworkShape/Temperature.lean`, **proved**). Two tokens with the same value
  can be stored as one that carries both weights, and the average does not change
  (**to prove**; **checked by hand**).
- **What it gives up.** The slope is that of everything after the cache, and layers
  multiply slope (chapter 28).

## E. A state instead of a context

### E1. Recurrent and state space models

- **Why it works.** The memory cell passes its content on through a gate. A gate that is
  open is a skip through time.
- **Proved.** `cell_slope_is_the_forget_gate`, `open_gate_is_a_skip_through_time`,
  `memory_gap_after_many_steps_is_product_of_gates` in
  `Supporting/NetworkShape/Residual.lean`.
- **What it gives up.** A state of fixed size is the bottleneck of D2. And the memory fades
  as the product of the gates, so remembering across `n` steps needs gates within about
  `1 / n` of one (**to prove**).

### E2. Carrying a cache between segments

- **Why it works.** Each layer reads the stored states of the segment before. The reach is
  then the number of layers times the segment length (B1), and it works only with relative
  positions (A1), because the stored states were computed at other positions.
- **To prove.** The reach, from the window theorem.

## F. The prompt

### F1. Saying it again, and putting the question last

- **Why it works.** A token that appears `k` times gets, in total, the share that one copy
  would get with `log k` added to its score (**to prove**; **checked by hand**). With a
  penalty on distance (A2), the last tokens get the largest shares.
- **Not explained.** That models attend less to the middle of a long context than to its
  ends. Nothing in the library says why.

### F2. Working in steps

- **Why it works.** Each written token sends the context through the layers once more. Many
  passes of a shallow model are a deep model, and depth computes with few units what one
  pass cannot.
- **Proved.** `deep_narrow_network_computes_parity` against
  `shallow_network_needs_one_unit_per_bit_less_one` in `Connections/ParityZigzag.lean`.
- **Note.** This is a strategy for long reasoning more than for long context. The author
  may want it left out.

## Already in the book, and needed by all of the above

A deep stack can carry a long context at all only because of its skips. Attention alone
merges the tokens at a geometric rate (`depth_merges_the_tokens_at_a_geometric_rate`), and a
skip keeps them apart (`residual_layer_merges_no_two_inputs`). Both are **proved** and are in
chapters 31 and 36.

## Common, but with nothing to prove here

- Faster exact attention (memory-efficient kernels, better hardware). The output is the
  same, so there is no "why" in this book's terms.
- Training or fine-tuning on longer sequences. The only statement is that lengths not seen
  in training are new inputs (Part VII).

## Left out as not common enough

My call; the author may overrule. Scores scaled with `log n` ("scalable softmax"), setting
gates from the length ("chrono initialisation"), token merging by similarity, random sparse
attention, locality-sensitive hashing.
